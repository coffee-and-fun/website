## What you are actually looking at

A Pokémon card is a paper sandwich: two printed layers with an opaque core between them. [Whitening is the core showing through](/blog/psa-whitening-pokemon-cards/) where the printed layer has been scraped, split, or crushed. Usually at a corner, usually along an edge, always worst on dark borders.

That matters for the fix question, because it tells you what kind of damage this is. The white is not on the card. The white *is* the card. There is no layer of dirt sitting on top of the ink that you could lift off, because nothing was added. Something was removed.

You cannot clean away an absence. Every "fix" that exists is therefore not a cleaning at all. It is adding something new to hide a gap, or cutting the gap off.

## The four things people try

Search around and you will find the same four suggestions, usually phrased as though they are trade secrets.

**Marker or paint on the white.** A fine black marker along a whitened border. It looks better for about a day, then the ink bleeds into the paper core, sits at a different sheen to the printed surface, and glows under a loupe.

**Trimming the frayed edge.** A guillotine or a blade to take off the damaged strip. It removes the whitening completely and it also makes the card smaller than every other copy of that card in existence.

**Bleach, whitener, or correction fluid on the borders.** The idea being that if the card has a white border anyway, you can make the whitening blend in. This is where the term "card doctoring" comes from.

**Pressing or rehydrating.** Damp cloth, heavy book, sometimes heat. This is aimed more at creases than whitening, but it comes up in the same threads, and it warps the card stock.

Every one of those has a matching entry in PSA's rejection list. Not by coincidence.

## PSA has a code for each of them

This is the part that changed how I think about the question. PSA's [grading standards](https://www.psacard.com/gradingstandards) include a set of "no grade" designations, which are not low grades. They are refusals. The card comes back unranked, in a holder that documents what was found.

| Code | PSA's wording | Which "fix" it catches |
|---|---|---|
| N1 | "Evidence of Trimming - When a card's edge appears to have been altered." | Trimming the frayed edge |
| N2 | "Evidence of Restoration - When a card's paper stock appears to have been built up." | Filling or rebuilding a corner |
| N3 | "Evidence of Recoloration - Where a card's color appears to have been artificially improved." | Marker, paint, touch-up |
| N5 | "Altered Stock - This includes, but is not limited to characteristics on the card that appear to show some form of alteration." | The catch-all |
| N7 | "Evidence of Cleaning - When a whitener is used to whiten borders or a solution is used to remove wax, candy, gum or tobacco stains." | Bleach and whitener |

Read N7 again. PSA wrote a dedicated rejection code for the specific act of using a whitener on a border. They did not write that code speculatively. They wrote it because people do this often enough to need a category for it.

That is the actual answer to "can you fix whitening". You can change how it looks. You cannot change what a grader finds, and the grader is looking for exactly the four things you would try.

## Why the fix is worse than the flaw

A whitened corner is ordinary wear. It costs you grades, it does not cost you the card. A PSA 6 with soft corners is a real card with a real market and a real price.

An altered card is a different object. It carries a permanent, documented note that someone worked on it. Nobody wants to be the person holding that when they try to sell, because the buyer's first question is what else was done. Whitening lowers a number. Alteration removes you from the market.

There is also the part nobody likes saying out loud. If you alter a card and then sell it without disclosing that, that is fraud, not a hobby technique. The line between "restoring" and "doctoring" is entirely about whether the next person knows.

## What you can actually do

Not nothing. Just less than you were hoping.

**Remove surface dirt, carefully.** A dry microfiber cloth, light pressure, front and back. This lifts dust and finger oil. It does not touch whitening, because whitening is not dirt. No liquids, no solvents, no eraser, no toothpaste.

**Stop it getting worse.** Whitening is cumulative and it never reverses. Penny sleeve, then a card saver or toploader, then leave it alone. Most of the damage on cards I have handled came from being taken in and out to be looked at.

**Grade it honestly, or don't grade it.** At [$79.99 for PSA's Regular service](https://www.psacard.com/services/tradingcardgrading) with a 40 to 50 business day turnaround, a card with visible corner whitening is usually not worth submitting at all. Work out what the card is worth at a 6 or a 7 first, using [the four things PSA actually grades on](/blog/how-psa-grades-pokemon-cards-research-guide/), before you spend anything.

**Sell it raw and say what it is.** Photograph the whitening, under good light, at an angle that shows it clearly. Buyers who want a clean copy will pass, which is fine, and buyers who want a playable or display copy will not care. An honest listing sells faster than a flattering one, because nobody opens the package and feels cheated.

**Keep it.** This is a real option that gets ignored. A card with a white corner is a card you can hold and look at without a plastic slab in the way.

## Where this argument gets shaky

Two honest caveats.

**Professional restoration is a real trade in other collectibles.** Comics, art, and coins all have restoration practices that are legitimate as long as they are disclosed, and the market prices restored items accordingly. Trading cards mostly do not work that way, but "never touch a card" is a norm of this hobby rather than a law of nature, and I am stating a norm.

**I have not tested any of this.** I am not going to take a marker to a card to find out how quickly it bleeds. Everything above about how the four techniques fail comes from PSA's own rejection categories and from what altered cards look like when they surface, not from an experiment I ran. If you want to break this post, that is where to push.

## The thought I keep landing on

The reason this question gets asked so often is that the flaw is small. A white speck on a corner feels like a fixable amount of damage. It is two millimetres of paper. Surely there is something.

There is not, and the reason is that grading is not measuring the card, it is measuring the card's history. Whitening is a record of something that happened to it. Every technique for removing that record leaves a bigger one.

The corner is not the problem. The corner is just where the card writes things down.
